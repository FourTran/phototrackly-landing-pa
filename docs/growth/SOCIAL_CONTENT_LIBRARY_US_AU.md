# LinkedIn founder posts

## 1 — The work around the shoot

A real-estate photography business can become operationally complicated long before it becomes a “big company.”

The camera work may be straightforward.

But around one property job you can have:

- booking
- a calendar
- photographer assignment
- property instructions
- uploads
- editor handoff
- quality checks
- delivery
- client questions

The interesting problem isn’t “how do we add more software?”

It’s: **how does everyone know what needs to happen next?**

That’s the problem I’m studying while building PhotoTrackly.

If you run a real-estate media team: where does one job usually become hardest to coordinate?

## 2 — Growth changes the job

Something I keep noticing while researching real-estate photography operations:

Growth changes the owner’s job.

At the beginning, you shoot.

Then you shoot + schedule.

Then you shoot + schedule + upload + check edits + answer messages + make sure delivery happened.

Then you add a photographer or editor to reduce workload… and create another handoff to manage.

Delegation solves capacity.

It doesn’t automatically solve coordination.

I’m curious how teams handle that transition today.

## 3 — One simple question

A question I think reveals a lot about an operations workflow:

**“How do you know this property job is actually on track?”**

Not “where is it booked?”

Not “where are the files?”

Actually on track.

For a growing real-estate media team, the answer may require checking a calendar, messages, storage, editing progress, and delivery status.

I’m exploring whether that decision can become much simpler.

## 4 — The editor handoff

Outsourcing editing can give a photography business much more capacity.

But it creates a new operational question:

**When is a job truly ready for the editor?**

Is every source file uploaded?

Are the instructions clear?

Who notices if something is missing?

And when the editor is finished, who owns QC?

That handoff is one of the workflows we’re studying for PhotoTrackly.

If you outsource editing, what part of the handoff still requires the most manual checking?

## 5 — Tool fragmentation without attacking tools

I don’t think the problem is that real-estate photographers use “bad tools.”

A calendar can be excellent at calendars.

Dropbox can be excellent at files.

A gallery can be excellent at delivery.

Messaging can be excellent at conversations.

The problem appears when one property job exists across all of them.

Then somebody has to reconstruct the state of the job in their head.

That coordination layer is what I’m interested in.

## 6 — Product principle

A product principle I’m using for PhotoTrackly:

**Don’t automate a workflow we don’t understand yet.**

It’s tempting to jump straight to AI assignment, automatic follow-ups, and autonomous operations.

But first we need to understand:

- what a good handoff looks like
- who has authority at each step
- what evidence means a job is complete
- where human judgment is still important

The first release is intentionally much more practical: clearer jobs, scheduling, production, QC, and delivery.

## 7 — A status is not enough

“Editing” is a status.

But it doesn’t answer:

- Who has the job?
- Did they accept it?
- Are all source files there?
- When is it expected back?
- Is QC next?
- Is anything blocking delivery?

I think useful operations software needs to make the **next responsibility** as clear as the current status.

## 8 — Research question

Real-estate media owners/operators:

Think about the last job where you had to chase someone.

What happened?

Was it:

1. photographer scheduling
2. missing/uploaded files
3. editor handoff
4. revision/QC
5. delivery
6. something else

I’m collecting real workflow examples while building PhotoTrackly. I’m much more interested in the messy real process than the ideal one.

## 9 — Pre-launch transparency

We’re building PhotoTrackly, but I don’t want to pretend the product is finished.

The current goal is simpler:

Talk to real-estate photography/media teams in the US and Australia.

Understand the daily workflow from booking → shoot → files → editing → QC → delivery.

Then make the first release solve a narrow set of those handoffs properly.

Early access is open for teams willing to share how they actually operate.

## 10 — Scale without “enterprise”

You don’t need 100 employees to have an operations problem.

A real-estate media company with:

- 3 photographers
- external editors
- several service types
- tight turnaround expectations

can already have a surprisingly complicated workflow.

The complexity comes from handoffs, not headcount.

That’s a useful lens for deciding when workflow software becomes valuable.

## 11 — What should stay manual?

One question I want every PhotoTrackly conversation to include:

**What should NOT be automated?**

Maybe photographer assignment needs judgment.

Maybe QC definitely does.

Maybe a high-value client needs special handling.

Automation is useful when the policy is clear.

Otherwise it just makes mistakes faster.

## 12 — Booking is only the beginning

A booking system can tell you a shoot exists.

That’s only the beginning of the operational story.

After booking:

→ Who is shooting it?
→ What services were ordered?
→ Are the files complete?
→ Who is editing?
→ Has QC happened?
→ Is delivery ready?

We’re designing PhotoTrackly around that full job lifecycle rather than treating booking as the finish line.

## 13 — US/Australia research

I’m focusing PhotoTrackly’s first customer research on two markets: the United States and Australia.

Not because I assume the workflows are identical.

The opposite.

I want to learn what stays consistent and what changes:

- terminology
- turnaround expectations
- team structure
- outsourcing
- service mix
- scheduling practices

If you operate in either market, I’d love to compare how your workflow actually runs.

## 14 — Production board question

If you had one production board for every property job, what are the minimum columns it would need?

My current hypothesis:

Booked → Scheduled → Uploaded → Editing → QC → Delivered

But real workflows rarely fit a neat diagram.

What stage is missing from yours?

## 15 — Not another gallery

PhotoTrackly isn’t being designed as another image editor or gallery.

The focus is the work **around** the media:

who needs to do what, what is waiting, what is missing, and what is ready to move forward.

There are already good tools for many individual steps.

I’m interested in making the overall job easier to operate.

## 16 — Early access ask

I’m looking for a small number of growing real-estate photography/media teams in the US and Australia for early PhotoTrackly conversations.

Good fit if you already have:

- multiple photographers or editors
- several services per property
- jobs moving through multiple tools
- an owner/coordinator still checking handoffs manually

This is pre-launch. I’m not looking for people to “buy now.”

I’m looking for operators willing to show me where the real workflow gets messy.

## 25 — Workflow stack discussion

**Title:** For teams with multiple photographers/editors: what’s your actual booking-to-delivery stack?

I’m trying to understand how growing real-estate photography teams run the whole job, not just booking.

For example:

- booking / order intake
- scheduling
- photographer assignment
- raw file upload
- editor handoff
- QC
- client delivery

Do you have one system that covers most of this, or is it still a combination of calendar + storage + messaging + gallery/etc.?

The part I’m especially curious about: when something is late or missing, where do you notice it first?

## 26 — Editor handoff discussion

**Title:** If you outsource editing, how do you manage the handoff and QC?

For people using external editors: what does your workflow look like from photographer upload to final delivery?

How does the editor know a job is ready?

How do you communicate special instructions?

What happens when files are missing or QC finds an issue?

Interested in what actually works at volume rather than the ideal process.

## 27 — Scaling discussion

**Title:** What operational task became painful first when your shoot volume increased?

For anyone who went from solo → multiple photographers/editors/coordinators, what was the first part of the business that stopped working smoothly?

Scheduling?

Keeping files organized?

Editing handoffs?

QC?

Delivery?

Admin/client communication?

I’m interested in the transition point where the old workflow stopped being enough.

## 28 — Production board discussion

**Title:** If you use a production board, what stages do you actually track?

I keep seeing variants of:

Booked → Scheduled → Shot → Uploaded → Editing → QC → Delivered

But I’m guessing the useful stages depend heavily on service mix and team size.

What stages/statuses are actually important in your business, and which ones just create admin overhead?

## 29 — Exception discussion

**Title:** How do you catch jobs that are quietly falling behind?

Not the obvious emergencies—the jobs that look fine until somebody realizes a file is missing, an editor never picked it up, or QC hasn’t happened.

Do you rely on people remembering, notifications, a board, a coordinator, or something else?

Curious about practical differences rather than photography style:

- booking lead time
- turnaround expectations
- who schedules photographers
- outsourcing editing
- common service bundles
- client delivery process

If you’ve worked in either market (especially both), what differences matter operationally?
